# I026 The Letter to Hardy

Working title: The Letter to Hardy. Subtitle: *Ramanujan, and a series that hunts down pi.*
Story after R. Kanigel, *The Man Who Knew Infinity* (1991), credited; not consulted directly.
Format: plate, about 3 minutes, Kokoro `af_heart`. Field: mathematics.

## Narration

1. Science Stories. The letter to Hardy.
2. Cambridge, early 1913. The mathematician G. H. Hardy opens a letter from India.
3. Inside are pages of formulas. More than a hundred results, and almost no proofs.
4. The letter begins: I beg to introduce myself to you as a clerk in the Accounts Department of the Port Trust Office at Madras, on a salary of only twenty pounds per annum.
5. The writer is Srinivasa Ramanujan. He says he is about twenty-three. By his birth date, he is in fact twenty-five.
6. He has no degree. He has already written to two other Cambridge mathematicians, Hobson and Baker. Neither replied.
7. So here is the puzzle Hardy faces. A stranger, with no training and no proofs. Is this genius, or nonsense?
8. The easy answer is to put the letter aside. Two professors already had.
9. Hardy does not. He sits down with his colleague, John Littlewood, and they go through it.
10. Some formulas they recognise. Some they can prove. And some, Hardy later wrote, defeated him completely. He had never seen anything in the least like them.
11. His verdict: they must be true, because if they were not true, no one would have had the imagination to invent them.
12. To see what kind of mind this was, look at what Ramanujan did with one of the oldest problems there is. Pi.
13. Pi can be written as an infinite sum. Here is a classic one: four, minus four thirds, plus four fifths, minus four sevenths, and on forever.
14. Each new term pulls the total back and forth across pi. But it closes in painfully slowly.
15. After ten terms, you have three point oh four. After a thousand terms, you still only have three point one four.
16. Now here is a series Ramanujan published in 1914, the year after his letter.
17. It looks like a monster. Factorials, the number eleven oh three, the number nine thousand eight hundred and one.
18. But watch what happens when we add it up.
19. The first term alone gives three point one four one five nine two. Seven correct digits.
20. Add a second term, and sixteen digits are right.
21. Every new term adds about eight more correct digits. Where the classic series crawls, this one leaps.
22. Ramanujan gave no proof of it. Seventy years later, in 1985, Bill Gosper used this series to compute more than seventeen million digits of pi.
23. And Hardy? He replied to the letter in February 1913, and in April 1914 Ramanujan arrived in England.
24. In 1918 he was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society. Two years later, back in India, he died. He was thirty-two.
25. Series built on his ideas are still used to chase digits of pi today.
26. Try it yourself. Open a spreadsheet. In one column, add up the classic series, term by term.
27. In another, add up Ramanujan's series. You will only need two rows. By then, the spreadsheet has run out of digits.
28. Then count how many digits each one gets right. One crawls. One leaps.
29. A clerk's letter, a reader willing to take it seriously, and an idea that still runs on our computers.

## Decisions

- The 1/pi series is not in the 1913 letter; the video says it was published in 1914, "the year after his letter".
- The 2/pi series sometimes said to be in the first letter is left out: not confirmed from the sources read.
- "Seventy years later" is 1914 to 1985 (71 years); said as "seventy years later, in 1985".
- Age: the letter says "about 23"; born 22 December 1887, so 25 on 16 January 1913 (arithmetic from the birth date).
- Hardy's "defeated me completely" quote is paraphrased in the third person.
- "Series built on his ideas are still used" is a general claim (Ramanujan-type series such as the Chudnovsky formula).
- Computations: classic (Gregory-Leibniz) series 4(1 - 1/3 + 1/5 - ...): 10 terms 3.0418, 1000 terms 3.14059. Ramanujan: k=0 gives 3.14159273 (error 7.6e-8), k=1 error 6.4e-16, k=2 error 5.7e-24 (computed with mpmath, 50 digits).
