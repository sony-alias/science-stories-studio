# I043 Nature Cannot Be Fooled

Working title in Ideas: *Ice Water and the Challenger O-Ring*.
Book: James Gleick, *Genius: The Life and Science of Richard Feynman* (1992), credited for the story; not consulted
directly in this run. Facts were checked against the Rogers Commission record and the pages below.
Format: one plate, about 3 min 50 s, 583 words of narration.

Care: the crew's loss is told plainly and briefly, with no breakup imagery, and the video closes "In memory of the
crew of Challenger, flight 51-L".

## Narration

1. Science Stories. Nature cannot be fooled.
2. Washington, the eleventh of February, 1986. At a public hearing sits a physicist, with a cup of ice water in front of him.
3. His name is Richard Feynman. He is one of the commissioners asked to find out why the space shuttle Challenger was lost.
4. Two weeks earlier, on the twenty-eighth of January, Challenger broke apart seventy-three seconds after launch.
5. All seven of its crew were killed.
6. Feynman's puzzle begins with something small: a ring of rubber, about seven millimetres thick.
7. Each of the shuttle's two solid rocket boosters was built from steel segments, joined end to end.
8. At every joint, a tongue of steel slots into a groove, and two rubber O-rings seal the gap between them.
9. Their job is to keep the burning gas inside, at the moment the rocket fires.
10. The obvious thought is that the cold should hardly matter. Chilled rubber does not shatter. It is still a ring, still in its groove.
11. But the joint does not stay still. When the motor lights, pressure swells the steel, and the gap at the joint opens.
12. Not by much, less than a millimetre. But it opens in a fraction of a second.
13. The seal only works if the rubber springs out and follows the moving steel.
14. So the question is not whether the rubber survives the cold. It is how fast it springs back. Engineers call this resilience.
15. Warm rubber is quick. It chases the gap and seals it.
16. Cold rubber is sluggish. For a moment the gap stays open, and hot gas can push past.
17. On the eve of the launch, engineers at the booster's maker, Morton Thiokol, were worried about exactly this.
18. The coldest launch so far had been at fifty-three degrees Fahrenheit, about twelve Celsius.
19. One of the engineers, Roger Boisjoly, summed up their view: we should not fly outside of our data base.
20. The launch went ahead. That morning the air at the pad was thirty-six degrees Fahrenheit, about two Celsius.
21. Infrared readings near the joint that failed suggested about twenty-eight, below freezing.
22. Back at the hearing, Feynman had chilled a squeezed piece of the seal's rubber in his ice water. Then he let it go.
23. In his words, the stuff looked as if it was less resilient at lower temperatures.
24. He admitted it was not the way to do such experiments. But anyone watching could see the point.
25. The commission found that the seal had failed in a joint of the right-hand booster. Hot gas escaped, and the shuttle broke apart.
26. In his own part of the report, Feynman asked how likely a shuttle was to fail. Working engineers said roughly one flight in a hundred.
27. Management said one in a hundred thousand: a launch every day for three hundred years, losing only one.
28. He ended with a sentence engineers still quote. For a successful technology, reality must take precedence over public relations, for nature cannot be fooled.
29. Try it yourself. Get two small rubber O-rings or tap washers from a hardware shop.
30. Squeeze each one flat in a binder clip. Put one in ice water and keep the other at room temperature, for ten minutes.
31. Release both, and measure their thickness with a ruler or caliper, at once and then every few seconds.
32. Plot thickness against time. The warm ring should recover faster. Try other rubbers too: some cope with cold far better than others.
33. A ring of rubber, a cup of ice water, and an honest question.

Computed and asserted at render time: 53 F = 12 C, 36 F = 2 C, 28 F = -2 C, 0.28 in = 7.1 mm, 0.029 in < 1 mm,
300 x 365 = 109,500 days (about 100,000).

Drawn qualitatively and labelled on screen: the shuttle, booster and joint (stylised, not to scale, gap exaggerated);
the warm and cold seal curves (illustrative, first-order lag model, not measured data); the room-temperature
comparison piece in the ice-water scene (an illustration; the record does not say he showed one); the try-it plot
("the shape you may see").

Uncertain and therefore worded carefully: the transcript text read says "in ice", APS says a cup of ice water; that
the piece was squeezed (commonly told as a C-clamp) is from general accounts and Feynman's own later telling, not from
the transcript pages read, so the video says "a squeezed piece" and shows generic jaws, not a C-clamp.
