# I019 The Bridge That Twisted Itself Apart

Story after Henry Petroski, *To Engineer Is Human: The Role of Failure in Successful Design* (1985), credited for
the story; the book was not consulted directly for this draft. Facts checked against the sources listed at the end.

Scene file: `episodes/I019_tacoma_flutter.py` (scene `TacomaFlutter`).

## Narration

1. Science Stories. The bridge that twisted itself apart.
2. November the seventh, 1940. Professor F. B. Farquharson arrives at the Tacoma Narrows Bridge with his cameras.
3. The bridge had opened that July. People already called it Galloping Gertie, because its deck bounced up and down in the wind.
4. Farquharson, an engineering professor at the University of Washington, had been studying that motion with scale models.
5. That morning the wind is strong, but steady: about forty miles an hour.
6. Just after ten o'clock, the motion changes.
7. The deck stops bouncing, and starts to twist.
8. Accounts say the roadway tilted as much as forty-five degrees, rocking back and forth about every five seconds.
9. A newspaper editor, Leonard Coatsworth, abandons his car on the bridge and crawls to safety. His daughter's dog, Tubby, is still inside.
10. Farquharson walks out to rescue the dog, but the terrified animal bites him.
11. Just after eleven, six hundred feet of roadway falls into the Narrows. No person dies, but Tubby is lost.
12. So what destroyed the bridge? Many textbooks say: resonance.
13. Push a swing in time with its rhythm, and every push adds a little more.
14. But resonance needs a push that repeats, in step with the bridge. The wind that morning was not a rhythm. It was a steady stream.
15. The board that investigated the collapse called resonance improbable.
16. Here is the deck in cross-section. Its edges are solid steel girders, eight feet deep. To the wind, it is a blunt, flat wing.
17. When the deck twists, the air peeling off its edges rolls into swirls, and the timing of those swirls is set by the deck's own motion.
18. So the wind's push arrives in step with the twist, every time. Nobody is pushing the bridge with a rhythm. The bridge makes its own.
19. Engineers call this flutter: a self-excited oscillation.
20. Write the twist as a spring with friction. Friction always takes energy out.
21. But the wind adds a term that depends on how fast the deck is turning. It acts like friction running backwards.
22. At low wind speed, friction wins, and any twist dies away.
23. Above a critical speed, the total damping turns negative. Each swing feeds the next, and the twist grows until something breaks.
24. Resonance is a swing pushed by a friend. Flutter is a swing that pushes itself, with a steady wind as its fuel.
25. Farquharson had seen this twist on his models. He had said that if it ever happened on the real bridge, it would be the end of the bridge.
26. The new bridge, opened in 1950, used deep, open trusses and grates in the roadway, so the wind passes through instead of swirling around a solid wall.
27. Since then, testing bridge models in wind tunnels has become standard practice. And aircraft designers guard against the same flutter in every wing.
28. Try it yourself. Cut a strip of card about forty centimetres long, and fold its long edges down, like Gertie's solid girders.
29. Hang it by its ends from loops of thread, so it can twist, and aim a fan at its edge.
30. Turn the fan up slowly, and watch for a twist that grows on its own.
31. Then make a second deck with windows cut in its sides, and see whether it stays steadier.
32. Keep fingers and thread well away from the fan blades.
33. A steady wind, a flat deck, and a bridge that pushed itself apart. Failure taught engineers to listen to the air.

## Notes on accuracy

- Opening 1 July 1940 (APS). Collapse 7 November 1940 (all sources).
- Wind 38 mph at 7:30 a.m., 42 mph later in the morning (WSDOT, STRUCTURE) — said as "about forty".
- Twisting began about 10:03 a.m. (WSDOT); "up to 45 degrees", "every 5 seconds" (WSDOT; STRUCTURE gives 45 degrees
  and 12 cycles per minute) — said as "witnesses said".
- 600-foot section fell at about 11:02 (WSDOT). No human deaths; Tubby lost (ASCE, FOX Weather).
- Coatsworth: news editor of the Tacoma News Tribune; the dog belonged to his daughter (WSDOT, ASCE).
- Farquharson bitten while trying to rescue the dog (WSDOT, ASCE, FOX Weather).
- 8-foot-deep plate girders (WSDOT, APS); the board found the solid girder and deck acted like an aerofoil (WSDOT).
- Federal Works Agency board called resonance "improbable" (APS).
- Self-excited torsional flutter / negative damping: WSDOT ("self-excited"), Billah and Scanlan 1991 via Ketchum.
- Farquharson's model remark paraphrased from the quotation on the WSDOT and APS pages.
- 1950 bridge: 33-foot-deep open trusses, roadway grates (STRUCTURE, APS).
- On screen, the twisting shape, the cross-section swirls and the damping curves are labelled as schematic
  illustrations, not measured data.
