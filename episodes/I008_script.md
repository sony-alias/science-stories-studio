# I008 · The Wind Tunnel in a Bicycle Shop

Book: David McCullough, *The Wright Brothers* (New York: Simon & Schuster, 2015). Told in our own words.
Field: Engineering. Scene file: `episodes/I008_wright_wind_tunnel.py`, scene `WrightWindTunnel`.

## Narration

1. Science Stories. The wind tunnel in a bicycle shop.
2. Summer, 1901. On the windy dunes of Kill Devil Hills, in North Carolina, two bicycle makers from Dayton, Ohio, are flying their new glider. Wilbur and Orville Wright.
3. They had designed it with the best numbers anyone had published. But it lifts only about a third of what those numbers promised.
4. Years later, Orville remembered Wilbur saying that men would not fly in a thousand years.
5. So where was the mistake? Every designer of the time worked from one equation for lift.
6. Lift equals a constant, times the wing's area, times the speed squared, times a lift coefficient that depends on the wing's shape and its angle to the wind.
7. The constant was more than a century old: point zero zero five. The lift coefficients came from tables published by the German glider pioneer Otto Lilienthal.
8. Either the glider was wrong, or the numbers were. So the brothers set out to test the numbers themselves.
9. That autumn, back in Dayton, they fixed a spare wheel flat on the handlebars of a bicycle, with a small curved wing on one side and a flat plate on the other.
10. Riding into the air, the two should have balanced, if Lilienthal's table was right. Instead the wheel swung round. The wing was giving far less lift than the table said.
11. The street was too rough a laboratory. So in the bicycle shop they built a wooden box, about six feet long and sixteen inches square, with a fan at one end driven by the shop's engine.
12. Air ran through it at roughly twenty-five to thirty miles an hour. Inside sat a balance made from bicycle spoke wire and old hacksaw blades.
13. The clever part: the balance set the lift of a test wing against the drag of flat plates. The old constant appeared on both sides, and cancelled. A pointer simply gave an angle, and the angle gave the lift coefficient.
14. Through the late autumn they tested around two hundred small metal wing shapes, and studied a few dozen of them in depth, angle after angle.
15. Curve by curve, a picture emerged. A long, narrow wing lifted more than a short, wide one of the same area.
16. And the old constant was wrong. The true value was nearer point zero zero three three. That alone made every prediction about one and a half times too high.
17. Lilienthal's tables, it turned out, were largely sound for his own wing. But the Wrights' wing was far shorter and wider for its area, and nobody had known how much that mattered.
18. In 1902 they went back to the dunes with a glider whose wings were about twice as long for their depth. That autumn they made somewhere between seven hundred and a thousand glides, the longest more than six hundred feet.
19. And on the seventeenth of December, 1903, they flew under power.
20. Wind tunnels have shaped aircraft ever since. The brothers' real invention was a habit: when the numbers disagree with the world, go and measure.
21. Try it yourself. Tape a long cardboard box open at both ends in front of a box fan. Hang a card wing on a ruler balance, and watch the reading change as you tilt the wing, or swap in a longer, narrower wing of the same area. Keep your fingers clear of the fan.

## On-screen notes

- The lift curves are schematic, drawn from modern thin-wing theory (lifting-line correction for aspect ratio); they are not the Wrights' measurements. This is labelled on screen.
- The bicycle rig is drawn schematically and labelled so; the angle of the test wing is not stated because the sources do not agree on one.
- Wilbur's "thousand years" remark is presented as Orville's later recollection, not a verbatim quotation. Other tellings give "not within our lifetime" or "fifty years".
- Tunnel airspeed is given as a range (about 25 to 30 mph) because sources differ (NPS: about 25 mph; Franklin Institute and USAF Museum: 25 to 35 mph).
- Number of shapes: Britannica says between 100 and 200; NPS says more than 200. Narration says "around two hundred".
- Aspect ratios: 1901 glider about 3.3; Lilienthal's test wing about 6.5; 1902 glider about 6.7 (NASM). "About twice as long for their depth" summarises 3.3 to 6.7.
- Silhouettes are stylised, not likenesses.
