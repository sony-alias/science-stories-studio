# I040 Time Is Position

Book: Dava Sobel, *Longitude* (1995), credited for the story; not consulted directly in this run.
Format: one plate, about 3 to 3.5 minutes.

## Narration

1. Science Stories. Time is position.
2. The evening of the twenty-second of October, 1707. Admiral Sir Cloudesley Shovell is bringing his fleet home from Gibraltar.
3. In the dark, off the Isles of Scilly, his ships strike the rocks.
4. Four go down. More than fourteen hundred men are lost, the admiral among them.
5. The story often told is that the navigators did not know their longitude.
6. Historians now point more to faulty charts and a mistake in latitude. But the longitude problem itself was real.
7. Latitude was easy. Measure how high the noon sun stands, and you know how far north you are.
8. East and west were different. Every point on a circle of latitude sees the same sky, just at a different time.
9. On the ninth of July, 1714, after lobbying by mathematicians like William Whiston, Britain offered up to twenty thousand pounds for a way to find longitude at sea.
10. The key idea was already old. In 1530 a young scholar from Friesland, Gemma Frisius, aged twenty-one, wrote it down.
11. Carry a clock set to the time at home. Wherever you are, compare it with the local time from the sun.
12. Here is why that works. The Earth turns once, three hundred and sixty degrees, in twenty-four hours.
13. That is fifteen degrees every hour, or one degree every four minutes.
14. So noon sweeps around the world from east to west. Each meridian gets its noon a little later.
15. Suppose the sun is highest at your ship, and your clock from home says three in the afternoon.
16. Noon at home was three hours ago. Three hours times fifteen degrees: you are forty-five degrees west of home.
17. Time difference becomes position.
18. But how good must the clock be? At the equator, one degree of longitude is about sixty nautical miles.
19. The top prize asked for half a degree: thirty miles, which is just two minutes of clock time.
20. Spread two minutes over a six-week voyage, and the clock may drift by only about three seconds a day.
21. Gemma saw the catch. It must be, he wrote, a very finely made clock which does not vary with change of air.
22. On a rolling ship, the best clocks of the day, pendulum clocks, were useless.
23. The idea was simple. The machine was the problem.
24. A carpenter who had taught himself clockmaking, John Harrison, would give decades of his life to that machine. That is our next story.
25. The idea never went away. A G P S receiver also finds where it is from time: from signals sent by satellites carrying atomic clocks.
26. Try it yourself. Push a straight stick upright into flat ground on a sunny day.
27. Every few minutes around midday, mark the tip of its shadow and note the time. The shortest shadow is your local noon.
28. Correct for the equation of time: through the year, the sun can run up to about sixteen minutes ahead of the clock, or about fourteen behind. Look it up for today's date.
29. In India, clock noon is the sun's noon at eighty-two and a half degrees east. Each four minutes later is one degree further west.
30. A stick, a watch, and the turning Earth. Time is position.

## Decisions

- Person at the centre: Gemma Frisius (1530) and his clock idea, framed by the 1707 Scilly wreck and the 1714 Act.
  Harrison is held back for I041.
- The popular claim that the 1707 wreck was caused by a longitude error (Sobel) is not supported by the Wikipedia
  article's sources (May 1960 and others point to latitude and charts), so the narration says so plainly.
- The Act's trial condition (a voyage to the West Indies) could not be read in a source, so the six-week voyage is
  given as an example of scale; the result (about 3 s a day) agrees with RMG's "three seconds a day" for Harrison's aim.
- Try it: written for an Indian viewer (IST meridian 82.5° E = 5.5 h x 15°), with the general rule on screen.

## Computed (asserted at render time)

- 360° / 24 h = 15° per hour = 1° per 4 minutes.
- 3 h x 15° = 45°.
- 1/2° = 2 minutes of time; 120 s / 42 days = 2.86 s per day ("about three seconds").
- 1° at the equator: 2π x 6371 km / 360 = 111.2 km ≈ 60 nautical miles (1 nmi = 1.852 km; 111.2 / 1.852 = 60.0).
- IST: UTC+5:30 = 5.5 x 15° = 82.5° E.
