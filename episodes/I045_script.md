# I045 Planck's Act of Desperation

Working title in Ideas: *Planck's Act of Desperation* (kept).
Book: Manjit Kumar, *Quantum: Einstein, Bohr and the Great Debate about the Nature of Reality* (2008), credited for
the story; not consulted directly in this run. Facts were checked against the pages listed below.
Format: one plate, about 3 min 59 s, 598 words of narration (a little over the 550-word guide, inside 4 minutes).
Field: Physics. The Idea has a *Try it yourself*, so the video ends with one.

## Narration

1. Science Stories. Planck's act of desperation.
2. Berlin, 1900. Max Planck is forty-two, a professor of physics, and a cautious man. He does not want a revolution.
3. His puzzle is the glow of hot things. Heat a bar of iron and it turns dull red, then orange, then nearly white.
4. For a perfect absorber, which physicists call a black body, the colours of the glow depend only on its temperature.
5. So there should be one formula for that glow, at every wavelength and every temperature. Planck wanted it.
6. Plot brightness against wavelength, and the measurements make a hump: rising from the short waves, peaking, then fading into the infrared.
7. Heat the body more, and the hump grows taller, and its peak slides toward shorter waves. That is why red hot becomes white hot.
8. Wilhelm Wien had a formula that matched the short-wavelength side very well.
9. But by October 1900, Heinrich Rubens and Ferdinand Kurlbaum, measuring deep in the infrared, found that Wien's formula came out too low.
10. The obvious answer came from classical physics. Treat the light as waves, and share the heat out evenly among them.
11. It agrees with experiment only at the longest waves, far off this chart. But shorter waves keep fitting in, without end. Give each its share, and the glow shoots to infinity.
12. Every warm object would blaze in ultraviolet. This was later called the ultraviolet catastrophe, though it was not what drove Planck.
13. Planck found a formula that bends between the two: classical at long wavelengths, Wien's at short ones.
14. He showed it to the German Physical Society on the nineteenth of October, 1900. It fit the data. But it was a guess, and he needed a reason.
15. For that, he turned to a counting method from Ludwig Boltzmann, an approach he had long hoped to avoid.
16. And he made a strange assumption. The tiny vibrating sources of light could not hold just any amount of energy. Only whole packets: one, two, three, never two and a half.
17. The size of a packet grows with frequency. E equals h times nu.
18. Now the trick. For long waves the packets are small, and the heat buys plenty of them.
19. For short waves one packet costs more than the heat can usually pay. Those waves are rarely excited, and stay nearly dark.
20. So the curve turns down instead of running away. Planck gave this derivation on the fourteenth of December, 1900.
21. Out of it came a new constant of nature, h. His value was within about one percent of today's.
22. Planck did not think he had found a truth about light. Years later he called it an act of despair: an explanation had to be found at any price.
23. For years he tried to fit the quantum back into classical physics. It would not go.
24. Others took it further. In 1905, Einstein argued that light itself comes in quanta. In 1918, Planck won the Nobel Prize for the discovery of energy quanta.
25. Today his constant is so trusted that, since 2019, it defines the kilogram.
26. Try it yourself. Watch the coil of an electric stove as it heats up, from a safe distance. Never touch it.
27. At first you can feel its heat but see nothing. That is infrared, the long-wave side of the hump.
28. Near five hundred and twenty-five degrees Celsius, a dull red glow appears. Hotter still, it brightens toward orange.
29. Even then, the hump peaks in the infrared, near three micrometres. You only see its short-wave tail.
30. A cautious man, a desperate guess, and a packet of energy that began quantum physics.

Computed and asserted at render time (see the top of the episode file): curves from Planck's, Wien's and the
Rayleigh–Jeans laws with 2019 SI constants; Wien/Planck ratio at 8 µm, 1500 K = 0.70; classical law within 6% of
Planck's at 100 µm; peak at 1000 K = 2.9 µm and at ~700 °C ≈ 3.0 µm; chance of one packet at 0.5 µm, 1500 K
(e^(−hν/kT) ≈ 1 in 210 million, said on screen as "about 1 in 200 million"); 525 °C = 798 K.

What is illustrative, and labelled so: the infrared dots are on the true curve, not Rubens and Kurlbaum's data; the
energy ladders are not to scale (short-wave rung really 19 kT up); silhouettes are stylised; the hot-iron colours
are a drawn ramp, not colorimetry. The October 1900 formula is shown with two fitted constants c₁, c₂; h and k
appear only with the December derivation.

Kept out or hedged: the often-told "Sunday 7 October visit and postcard" story (not in the pages read); who coined
"ultraviolet catastrophe" and when (usually credited to Ehrenfest, 1911, not confirmed here, so the video only
says "later"); the German wording of the Wood letter (not seen; the English rendering is labelled a translation).
